CHAPTER 33: Blissful
David is deeply in love with Dora Spenlow and saturated with thoughts of her. He makes night-time pilgrimages to her house at Norwood, declaring his devotion dramatically. David confides his love to Peggotty, who is completely supportive. At the office, David encounters Mr. Murdstone, who has come to obtain a marriage license. Murdstone is remarrying, and a tense exchange occurs between him and Peggotty, who accuses him of destroying David's mother. During a planned picnic for Dora's birthday, David is ecstatic to spend time with her. Miss Mills, Dora's romantic friend, facilitates their courtship. David brings flowers and rides alongside the carriage, completely absorbed in Dora. At the picnic, he becomes jealous of "Red Whisker," another guest who pays attention to Dora. Miss Mills intervenes to reconcile them with melodramatic pronouncements about love. David retrieves Dora's guitar and sits with her, feeling triumphant. The memorable passage occurs when David describes being "steeped in Dora"—not merely over head and ears in love, but completely saturated. Later at Miss Mills's house, David proposes to Dora, overwhelming her with declarations that he would die without her. She accepts, and they become secretly engaged. Their engagement must remain hidden from her father, Mr. Spenlow, creating a delicious conspiracy facilitated by Miss Mills.

CHAPTER 34: My Aunt Astonishes Me
David writes to Agnes about his engagement to Dora, feeling emotional as he composes the letter. Her warm reply comforts him. Traddles visits David's chambers, having befriended Peggotty. Mrs. Crupp, David's landlady, resigns her duties in protest of Peggotty's presence and writes an indignant letter about "spies" and "intruders." Traddles helps David purchase his possessions back from a broker, including his flowerpot for his beloved Sophy. When David returns home, he discovers his Aunt Betsey and Mr. Dick have unexpectedly arrived in London with all their belongings. After initial pleasantries, Aunt Betsey delivers shocking news: she is ruined financially. She has lost everything through bad investments—mining ventures, treasure-hunting schemes, and finally a failed bank abroad. All her money is gone except what fits in her room and the cottage at Dover, which she's letting to Janet. She handles the crisis with characteristic determination, declaring they must "meet reverses boldly" and "live misfortune down." The memorable line comes when she sits upon her luggage, explaining "I prefer to sit upon my property" because "it's all I have." Despite her reduced circumstances, she maintains her dignity and immediately begins planning practical arrangements. David is devastated, realizing this changes everything about his prospects with Dora and his future.

CHAPTER 35: Depression
David struggles with the implications of his aunt's ruin, particularly how it affects his engagement to Dora. He must now earn his own living during his articled clerkship, when he receives no income. Mr. Dick is installed in Peggotty's former chandler's shop lodging, cheerfully accepting his humble accommodation. David attempts to cancel his articles with Mr. Spenlow and recover the premium, but both Mr. Spenlow and the fearsome Mr. Jorkins refuse, each blaming the other for the impossibility. Despondent, David encounters Agnes, who has come to London. She lifts his spirits immediately—her presence brings him "peace and happiness." Agnes is staying with her father and Uriah Heep, who have become business partners. She reveals that the Heeps now live in David's old room at the Wickfields' house, casting a shadow over her home. At David's chambers, Aunt Betsey reveals the full story of her financial ruin: bad advice led her to invest in foreign mining, diving for treasure, and ultimately a failed bank that paid nothing. She absolves Mr. Wickfield of blame, much to Agnes's relief. Agnes suggests David seek work as secretary to Dr. Strong, who has retired to Highgate. David writes immediately to the Doctor, and Agnes helps arrange his aunt's belongings in the chambers. The chapter memorably ends with a beggar crying "Blind! Blind! Blind!" after David parts from Agnes.

CHAPTER 36: Enthusiasm
David awakens with renewed determination to support his aunt through hard work. He walks to Highgate to meet with Dr. Strong, thinking enthusiastically about supporting Dora. The Doctor warmly receives him and offers seventy pounds annually to help with the Dictionary. This would double David's income. David is delighted, though disturbed to find Mr. Jack Maldon has returned from India due to "health reasons" and obtained a comfortable sinecure through the Doctor's generosity. Mrs. Strong seems troubled by Maldon's presence. David begins working mornings and evenings on the Dictionary, finding Mr. Maldon's previous amateur contributions more hindrance than help. Meanwhile, David determines to learn parliamentary shorthand reporting to earn additional income. Traddles warns this is extremely difficult—"equal to mastering six languages"—but David resolves to do it anyway for Dora's sake. To help Mr. Dick feel useful after the financial crisis, Traddles suggests he copy legal documents. They devise a system where Mr. Dick can insert passages about King Charles I into his Memorial when the urge strikes, while copying legal work for pay. This succeeds brilliantly, and Mr. Dick proudly earns ten shillings ninepence his first week. The Micawbers are leaving for Canterbury, where Mr. Micawber will work as Uriah Heep's confidential clerk—news that troubles David, though he cannot say why.

CHAPTER 37: A Little Cold Water
David maintains his rigorous work schedule and visits Dora at Miss Mills's house. When he tries to discuss their future and domestic economy, mentioning that she should learn about housekeeping and accounts, Dora becomes hysterical with fear. She cannot bear him being "practical" or "dreadful." David's attempts to give her a cookery book and teach her household management fail completely—the book gives her headaches, and she uses the tablets for drawing pictures of him and Jip instead. Miss Mills intervenes, explaining that Dora is "a thing of light, and airiness, and joy" unsuited to such practical matters. The memorable scene occurs when David tries to discuss their poverty: Dora, associating "beggar" only with grotesque street figures, cannot comprehend that he is poor. When he persists about crusts and hard work, she cries "Oh, don't, don't!" and worries only that "Jip must have a mutton-chop every day at twelve, or he'll die." David buys the cookery book anyway, but its principal use becomes serving as a stand for Jip to perform tricks. David realizes Dora is fundamentally impractical but loves her even more for her childish, winning ways. He becomes increasingly concerned that he cannot share his serious thoughts and anxieties with her, but he keeps these worries to himself.

CHAPTER 38: A Dissolution of Partnership
Mr. Spenlow unexpectedly summons David to a coffee-house, where Miss Murdstone has intercepted all of David's love letters to Dora. The letters, tied with blue ribbon, are presented as evidence of their secret engagement. Mr. Spenlow is outraged at this "stealthy and unbecoming action" and David's abuse of his hospitality. David defends his love for Dora but cannot deny the concealment. Mr. Spenlow offers to end the matter if David will burn the letters and forget Dora, but David refuses, declaring his love is above all earthly considerations. Mr. Spenlow warns that his will provisions for Dora could change if this "folly" continues, hinting at disinheritance. He demands David take a week to reconsider. The very next morning, shocking news arrives: Mr. Spenlow has died suddenly. He was found a mile from home after falling from his phaeton, either from a fit or after getting out while ill. The office is in chaos. David feels guilty and strange about their conflict ending this way. Weeks pass with Dora in mourning at Putney with her maiden aunts. Miss Mills keeps a melodramatic journal chronicling Dora's grief. When Mr. Jorkins and Tiffey search for Mr. Spenlow's will, they find nothing—despite his assurances, he died intestate. His affairs are in complete disorder, and his estate, after debts, is worth almost nothing. Dora is left penniless except for her two aunts' care.

CHAPTER 39: Wickfield and Heep
David visits Canterbury to see Agnes and deliver a letter to Dora's aunts proposing formal courtship. At the Wickfield house, he finds Mr. Micawber installed in Uriah's old office, pompously working as Uriah's clerk. Micawber drops hints about being in Heep's power financially. David finds Agnes in her old room, and she provides wise counsel about approaching Dora's aunts honestly. Visiting Mr. Wickfield's study, David sees the dramatic reversal: Uriah in power, Mr. Wickfield dependent and broken. Uriah has taken over the best rooms and reduced Mr. Wickfield to a shadow. Uriah's mother now lives in the house, constantly watching Agnes. Uriah takes David for a walk and reveals his jealousy over Mrs. Strong and Mr. Maldon, threatening to interfere in their friendship. The memorable confrontation occurs when Uriah confesses his "umble" origins—how he was taught at a foundation school to be subservient, but learned to use humility as a weapon to gain power. At dinner, Uriah gets Mr. Wickfield drunk and proposes a toast to Agnes, declaring his intention to marry her. Mr. Wickfield erupts in anguish, revealing Uriah's systematic destruction of him. David intervenes to calm Mr. Wickfield. Later, Agnes bids David goodnight, making him promise not to sacrifice himself to misguided duty. The next morning, Uriah smugly tells David that he and Mr. Wickfield have reconciled.

CHAPTER 40: The Wanderer
David's aunt is deeply troubled by the news from Canterbury and paces through the night. David writes to Dora's aunts and receives his aunt's approval. One snowy evening, David encounters Mr. Peggotty in London, searching for Emily. They meet by chance outside a church, and David recognizes Martha nearby. Mr. Peggotty explains his extensive search across Europe—through France, Switzerland, and Italy—always on foot or in poor coaches, asking after Emily everywhere. The memorable passage describes how foreign villagers, especially mothers who had lost daughters, sheltered him and helped him. He followed Steerforth to Switzerland but arrived too late; they had already gone. He returned home after months abroad, still determined to find Emily. Mr. Peggotty shows David the money Emily has sent in remorse—first fifty pounds, then letters with more money, heartbreakingly begging for news of her uncle and Ham. The letters reveal her anguish and shame. A third package contains ten pounds from a "true friend," postmarked from the Upper Rhine, giving Mr. Peggotty a new direction to search. He describes Ham's condition: working tirelessly, well-loved in Yarmouth, but with a deep wound and a reckless disregard for his own safety in dangerous situations. Before parting, David sees Martha's face at the door—she had been listening to the entire conversation, though Mr. Peggotty doesn't see her. David carefully leads Mr. Peggotty away without revealing her presence.

CHAPTER 41: Dora's Aunts
Dora's aunts, Miss Lavinia and Miss Clarissa, respond to David's letter, inviting him to call with a confidential friend. David brings Traddles, whose upright hair causes some concern. The two elderly sisters, like birds, receive them formally. Miss Lavinia, the authority on romance due to a supposed admirer named Pidger, leads the discussion. Miss Clarissa constantly interrupts with grievances about Dora's mother excluding them from family dinners. The aunts acknowledge that David and Dora's "light inclinations" might be genuine and grant permission for visits, but only to themselves initially, not recognizing any engagement. David must promise no secret communication with Dora. After deliberation, the sisters permit David to visit for Sunday dinner and twice-weekly for tea. David is ecstatic. Miss Lavinia then brings him to see Dora, who is hiding behind a door with Jip muffled in the plate-warmer. Their reunion is joyful. Dora is terrified of Traddles and begs David not to bring "that naughty, mischief-making old thing" Aunt Betsey. The memorable scene occurs when Dora appears in her wedding dress for David to preview. Regular visits begin, and eventually Aunt Betsey and the aunts establish cordial relations, though Jip never accepts Aunt Betsey and must be muffled during her visits. David worries that everyone treats Dora like "a pretty toy or plaything" rather than a responsible adult, but his attempts to discuss this with her fail.

CHAPTER 42: Mischief
David reflects on his hard work and dedication to succeeding for Dora's sake—the foundation of his later success. Agnes comes to visit Dr. Strong for a fortnight, and suspiciously, Mrs. Heep soon follows, supposedly for her rheumatism, with Uriah attending her. Uriah confronts David in the Doctor's garden, revealing his jealousy of Mrs. Strong and making sinister insinuations about her relationship with Mr. Maldon. He threatens to "put a stop" to Mrs. Strong's friendship with Agnes. David arranges for Agnes to meet Dora, a momentous occasion. Dora is initially afraid Agnes is "too clever" but is immediately won over by Agnes's gentle warmth. The tea is delightful, and later Agnes speaks lovingly to David about Dora. The memorable passage occurs when Dora wonders if having Agnes as a friend earlier might have made her "more clever," touching David deeply. That evening, David accidentally discovers Uriah, Mr. Wickfield, and the Doctor in the study. Uriah has told Dr. Strong about supposed impropriety between Mrs. Strong and Mr. Maldon, based on Mr. Wickfield's old suspicions and David's own observations. The Doctor is devastated but responds with characteristic nobility, declaring his complete faith in his wife's honor while acknowledging he may have "unwittingly ensnared" her into an unhappy marriage. David, enraged, strikes Uriah across the face, though Uriah maddeningly refuses to quarrel and "forgives" him, making David feel worse than if he had retaliated.

CHAPTER 43: Another Retrospect
In a brief, dreamlike chapter, David reflects on time passing as seasons blur together. He has reached twenty-one and achieved professional success. He has mastered shorthand and reports parliamentary debates for a newspaper, becoming disillusioned with politics: "I wallow in words. Britannia, that unfortunate female, is always before me, like a trussed fowl." Traddles has also tried reporting but isn't suited for it; he's been called to the bar instead. David has begun writing fiction secretly, publishing pieces in magazines and earning regular payment. He and Aunt Betsey move to a cottage near Highgate. The memorable passage occurs when David announces: "My marriage? Yes!" Dora's aunts flutter about wedding preparations, constantly summoning Dora for fittings while David and Agnes select furniture. Peggotty comes to help, and David sometimes sees Mr. Peggotty searching London's dark streets for Emily. David obtains the marriage license with Traddles as witness. Sophy arrives to be bridesmaid alongside Agnes. The wedding day arrives in a beautiful blur: David remembers fragments—the church, Miss Lavinia crying in memory of Pidger, Dora trembling, Peggotty signing the register after witnessing David's mother's wedding long ago. The dreamlike ceremony concludes with David and his "dear, dear, little wife" driving away together. David finally "awakens from the dream" and believes in his marriage's reality.

CHAPTER 44: Our Housekeeping
David discovers married life is strange after courtship's end—having Dora always present removes the delicious occupation of making love. They prove utterly incompetent at housekeeping. Their first servant, Paragon, with a Lifeguards cousin who growls constantly, turns out to be a disaster, found drunk under the boiler. A succession of servants follows, each worse than the last. The page boy they employ becomes a source of continual trouble—quarreling with cooks, growing alarmingly, and eventually stealing Dora's watch, confessing to numerous household thefts before being transported. David tries discussing household management with Dora, but she weeps, saying he's being "cruel" and "cross." Aunt Betsey intervenes, advising David never to criticize Dora, explaining "Little Blossom is a very tender little blossom" and sharing her own marriage regrets. The memorable scene occurs during their first quarrel when Dora accuses David of comparing her to the transported page. They reconcile, but domestic chaos continues. When Traddles visits for dinner, the oysters can't be opened, and Jip disrupts everything. Dora touchingly asks David to call her his "child-wife," explaining: "When you are going to be angry with me, say to yourself, 'it's only my child-wife!'" David accepts this, trying to bear all their household cares himself while keeping Dora happy, though he sometimes feels "the old unhappy loss or want of something" and wishes his wife were his counselor.

CHAPTER 45: Mr. Dick Fulfils My Aunt's Predictions
Mrs. Markleham, the "Old Soldier," constantly takes Mrs. Strong to amusements supposedly for her benefit but really for her own pleasure, while Dr. Strong grows sadder. Aunt Betsey believes Mr. Dick has a plan to help. One evening, Mr. Dick confides to David that he understands the Doctor and Mrs. Strong's problem: there's an "unhappy cause of separation" but the Doctor loves his wife devotedly. As someone "simple" whom no one minds, Mr. Dick can intervene where others cannot. Later, David and his aunt visit the Doctor's cottage, where Mrs. Markleham announces she caught the Doctor making his will, leaving everything "unconditionally" to Mrs. Strong. She praises this effusively. Mr. Dick suddenly leads Mrs. Strong into the study, where she falls to her knees before the Doctor with that "memorable look" David has seen before. In a long, emotional speech, Mrs. Strong reveals her whole heart: she married the Doctor out of gratitude and deep respect, seeing him as father and guide. Her mother first suggested mercenary motives, which poisoned her happiness. She had been fond of her cousin Maldon in childhood, but realized marriage to him would have been miserable—"There can be no disparity in marriage like unsuitability of mind and purpose." She knew Maldon's false heart when he made improper advances, which she never revealed out of shame. Her love for the Doctor was "founded on a rock" and endures. The reconciliation is complete, with Mr. Dick receiving credit for his wisdom, while the Old Soldier is defeated.

CHAPTER 46: Intelligence
Walking past Mrs. Steerforth's gloomy house, David is summoned by Miss Dartle. In the garden, she coldly informs him that Emily has run away from Steerforth. Littimer, Steerforth's servant, appears and condescendingly narrates events: Steerforth took Emily across Europe, where she was much admired. Eventually Steerforth tired of her moods, and she tired of his restlessness. He abandoned her near Naples, leaving Littimer to inform her and offer marriage to Littimer himself as "a very respectable person" who would overlook her past. Emily violently refused, escaped through a window, and vanished—possibly drowned. The memorable scene shows David's barely-controlled rage at Littimer's smug respectability. Mrs. Steerforth appears, greatly aged, explaining she paid Littimer for this information to warn Mr. Peggotty and prevent Emily from ensnaring Steerforth again. She acknowledges David's success as a writer and dismisses him coldly. David observes the two women gazing at the darkening prospect, with mist rising "like a sea" that seems to encompass them—a foreboding image. David finds Mr. Peggotty in his Hungerford Market lodging, kept ready for Emily's return. David shares the news. Mr. Peggotty is devastated but insists Emily lives: "I am told as she's alive!" David suggests enlisting Martha's help, knowing Emily had been charitable to her and that Martha would search London's streets effectively. They find Martha by the river, contemplating suicide, crying "Oh, the river!" Martha says the river haunts her—it's "the only thing in all the world that I am fit for." Mr. Peggotty asks her to search for Emily, offering complete trust. Martha solemnly vows to dedicate herself to this task, seeing it as potential redemption.

CHAPTER 47: Martha
David and Mr. Peggotty follow Martha through Westminster to the Thames riverside near Millbank Prison. The setting is memorably bleak: a "dreary" wasteland with rotting unfinished houses, rusty industrial refuse, slimy gaps, and "the rags of last year's handbills offering rewards for drowned men." Martha stands at the river's edge in despair. They approach carefully, and David calls her name. She screams and struggles but recognizes Mr. Peggotty. Sitting among stones, she repeatedly cries "Oh, the river!"—seeing it as her destiny: "It comes from country places, where there was once no harm in it—and it creeps through the dismal streets, defiled and miserable—and it goes away, like my life, to a great sea, that is always troubled." She insists she never harmed Emily and would have died to save her reputation. Mr. Peggotty gently explains they believe in her innocence and need her help finding Emily in London. Martha, astonished at being trusted, vows to search faithfully, declaring this task as her salvation from the river. She refuses money, saying it would "take away the only certain thing that saves me from the river." They part, and Martha walks away along the desolate road. David and Mr. Peggotty follow at a distance until reaching populated streets. Arriving home, David finds a strange man in Aunt Betsey's garden—her supposedly dead husband, demanding money. Aunt Betsey gives him what she can. After he leaves, she reveals the truth to David: she married unwisely, he ruined her, she left him generously, and now periodically pays him to stay away from England.

CHAPTER 48: Domestic
David's first novel succeeds, and he leaves parliamentary reporting forever. Their housekeeping remains chaotic. They employ a page boy who constantly quarrels with the cook and eventually steals Dora's watch, making endless penitent confessions about household thefts before being transported to Australia. David tries discussing their "want of system" with Dora, but she thinks he's comparing her to the transported page and dissolves in tears. David then attempts to "form her mind" by reading Shakespeare and discussing serious subjects, but this only depresses and frightens Dora, who thinks "Shakespeare a terrible fellow." Traddles becomes an unwitting accomplice when David "explodes mines upon him for the edification of Dora at second hand." After months of failure, David realizes "Dora's mind was already formed" and abandons the effort. He buys her earrings and tells her he's been wrong to try making her wise. Dora lovingly accepts, asking if he won't mind "things going a tiny morsel wrong, sometimes." David resolves to accept his child-wife as she is. The memorable reflection comes when David examines his heart: he loves Dora dearly and is happy, but "the happiness I had vaguely anticipated, once, was not the happiness I enjoyed, and there was always something wanting." Mrs. Strong's words haunt him: "the first mistaken impulse of an undisciplined heart" and "there can be no disparity in marriage like unsuitability of mind and purpose." The chapter ends ominously: Dora grows weak, unable to walk, though remaining cheerful. David begins carrying her up and down stairs daily, noticing she grows lighter in his arms.
