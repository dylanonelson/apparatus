CHAPTER 17: XVII: Somebody Turns Up
David writes to Peggotty from Dover and Doctor Strong's school in Canterbury. He learns that the Murdstones have left and his childhood home is shuttered. Mr. Dick visits David regularly on Wednesdays, becoming beloved by the schoolboys despite his eccentricity. Mr. Dick reveals a mysterious man who frightens Aunt Betsey and to whom she secretly gives money in the moonlight. David suspects this may involve attempts to remove Mr. Dick from his aunt's protection. One day, David is invited to tea at Uriah Heep's humble dwelling, where Uriah and his mother persistently extract information about Mr. Wickfield and Agnes through their obsequious questioning. The visit becomes uncomfortable as David realizes he's revealed more than intended. Suddenly, Mr. Micawber appears at the door—the memorable character from David's childhood. Micawber is as eloquent and impecunious as ever, speaking of "something turning up" while residing temporarily in Canterbury with his wife. After a convivial dinner together featuring punch and reminiscences, Micawber sends David a characteristic despairing letter announcing financial ruin, only for David to see him the next morning departing cheerfully on the London coach, eating walnuts. This chapter reunites David with colorful figures from his past while introducing the unsettling dynamic of Uriah Heep's manipulation.

CHAPTER 18: XVIII: A Retrospect
This poetic, impressionistic chapter captures David's school years through a series of vignettes rather than linear narrative. David recalls his early infatuation with Miss Shepherd, a boarding-school girl he adores from afar, eventually kissing her in the cloakroom before their relationship ends when she prefers another boy. He describes fighting the local butcher to defend Doctor Strong's honor, suffering a beating but earning Agnes's sympathetic care. Time passes swiftly—Adams, once head boy, becomes a barrister; David himself becomes head boy. Agnes transforms from child to woman, becoming his "sweet sister" and counselor. David, now wearing a gold watch and using bear's grease in his hair, falls desperately in love with the eldest Miss Larkins, a woman of thirty. He spends agonized hours outside her house, fantasizes about rescuing her from fire, and thrills when she dances a waltz with him at a ball, giving him a flower. His romantic dreams shatter when Agnes announces Miss Larkins's engagement to Mr. Chestle, a hop-grower. Dejected, David throws away the faded flower, fights the butcher again (this time victoriously), and resumes wearing his ring. The chapter memorably captures the intensity of adolescent emotions, the acceleration of time, and David's progression toward young manhood, all filtered through the haze of memory.

CHAPTER 19: XIX: I Look About Me, and Make a Discovery
David's schooldays end, and he must choose a profession. After deliberations with Aunt Betsey and Mr. Dick (who suggests "Brazier"), his aunt proposes he become a proctor in Doctors' Commons. She sends David on a preliminary trip to Yarmouth to visit Peggotty and gain perspective. In Canterbury, he bids farewell to Agnes, who warns him against Steerforth as his "bad Angel," though David defends his friend passionately. Agnes reveals troubling news: Uriah Heep is becoming her father's partner. She explains that Uriah has made himself indispensable, mastering Mr. Wickfield's weaknesses and creating a situation where her father is "afraid of him." Agnes accepted the partnership to relieve her father's distress, though she grieves over his deterioration. At Doctor Strong's, David learns of plans for the Doctor's retirement and hears concerning news about Annie Strong's cousin, Jack Maldon, returning from India due to illness. David observes Mr. Wickfield watching Annie Strong with suspicious intensity and recalls a troubling scene from Maldon's departure. David begins to doubt the innocence of the Doctor's young wife. Leaving Canterbury with these dark suspicions clouding his memory of his happy schooldays, David travels to London, where he unexpectedly encounters Steerforth at the inn, joyfully renewing their friendship.

CHAPTER 20: XX: Steerforth’s Home
David accompanies Steerforth to Highgate, where he meets Steerforth's proud, devoted mother and the sharp-tongued Rosa Dartle, a poor relation living with the family. Rosa bears a scar on her lip from a hammer that Steerforth threw at her in boyhood anger. David is attended by Littimer, Steerforth's supremely respectable manservant, whose presence makes David feel excruciatingly young and inadequate. Rosa Dartle displays a peculiar manner of speaking—hinting rather than stating directly, making insinuating observations that often have a sarcastic edge. She questions Mrs. Steerforth about university life and later interrogates David about Mr. Peggotty's family, wondering if such "common people" are "really animals and clods." Steerforth dismisses this, claiming such people are "not easily wounded" due to their "coarse rough skins." Rosa makes a cutting remark about how "consoling" it is to know that when such people suffer, "they don't feel." The evening reveals the complex dynamics of the household: Mrs. Steerforth's overwhelming devotion to her son, Rosa's barely concealed intense feelings toward Steerforth, and the undercurrents of class consciousness. David retires to find Rosa Dartle's portrait in his room, her scar seeming to come and go in the shadows, creating an unsettling presence that troubles his sleep.

CHAPTER 21: XXI: Little Em’ly
David and Steerforth spend a fortnight in Yarmouth. David introduces Steerforth to the Peggotty household, and their arrival coincides with joyous news: Little Em'ly has agreed to marry Ham after initially refusing him. Mr. Peggotty radiates happiness, and the household celebrates. Steerforth charms everyone with his easy manners and natural grace. He sings beautifully, tells captivating stories, and even rouses the melancholy Mrs. Gummidge to cheerfulness. David observes Em'ly watching Steerforth with fascinated attention while keeping some distance from Ham. When they leave, Steerforth calls Em'ly "an engaging little Beauty" and describes the gathering as "quite a new sensation." He makes a dismissive comment about Ham being "rather a chuckle-headed fellow," which briefly shocks David, but Steerforth laughingly deflects any criticism. David passionately defends Steerforth's genuine sympathy for common people, despite what he says to Miss Dartle. Steerforth responds enigmatically: "Daisy, I believe you are in earnest, and are good. I wish we all were!" The chapter introduces Littimer, Steerforth's manservant, whose respectability makes everyone feel inferior. Steerforth reveals he has bought a boat to be captained by Mr. Peggotty and renamed the "Little Em'ly." David interprets this as characteristic generosity, though modern readers may detect more sinister motives. The chapter ends with foreboding undertones beneath the surface cheerfulness.

CHAPTER 22: XXII: Some Old Scenes, and Some New People
David and Steerforth's visit continues, with the friends separating frequently—Steerforth sailing with Mr. Peggotty while David makes melancholy pilgrimages to Blunderstone, visiting his parents' grave and viewing his ruined childhood home. One dark evening, David finds Steerforth alone in brooding contemplation. Steerforth erupts in uncharacteristic despair, wishing he had "a judicious father" and could "guide myself better," declaring he'd rather be "this poor Peggotty" than himself. The mood passes quickly and Steerforth laughs it off. David and Steerforth encounter a mysterious, haggard woman following Ham and Em'ly. At dinner, they meet Miss Mowcher, an eccentric dwarf who works as hairdresser and cosmetician to the wealthy. She's witty, sharp, and volatile. When Steerforth describes Em'ly in detail—her name, occupation, engagement to Ham, her beauty—Miss Mowcher responds knowingly. Later, David encounters Ham outside Mr. Barkis's house. Ham explains Em'ly is inside with Martha Endell, a fallen woman and former friend desperately wanting to escape Yarmouth's shame for London. After Martha leaves, Em'ly weeps bitterly, expressing her fears about not being good enough: "I am not as good a girl as I ought to be!" She clings to Peggotty and asks everyone to help her be better, revealing her inner turmoil. The chapter ends with Em'ly clinging to Ham as they walk home in the moonlight—a moment of apparent security that feels ominously fragile.

CHAPTER 23: XXIII: I Corroborate Mr. Dick, and Choose a Profession
David resolves to become a proctor after Steerforth describes Doctors' Commons as a "lazy old nook" dealing with ecclesiastical law, wills, and maritime disputes. During a London visit with Aunt Betsey, a disturbing incident occurs: a threatening man approaches them on the street, and David's aunt frantically orders David away, goes off with the stranger, and returns having given him all her money. This corroborates Mr. Dick's mysterious observations. David and his aunt visit the offices of Spenlow and Jorkins to arrange his articles. Mr. Spenlow, extremely starched and formal, explains the premium is £1,000, blaming the inflexible "Mr. Jorkins" for any difficult terms—though David later learns Jorkins is merely a device to deflect complaints. David tours the Consistory Court, finding it sleepy and antiquated, filled with Doctors in wigs arguing obscure ecclesiastical cases. Aunt Betsey arranges furnished chambers for David in Buckingham Street, Adelphi, where the previous tenant died of drink and smoke. Mrs. Crupp, the landlady, will provide services. David moves in, delighted with his independence. The transition from schoolboy to young professional is underway, though David feels overwhelmed by the ancient, arcane world of Doctors' Commons. His aunt departs for Dover, leaving David to navigate London life alone. The chapter marks David's entry into adulthood and professional life, though he remains very young and inexperienced despite his new responsibilities.

CHAPTER 24: XXIV: My First Dissipation
David revels in his new independence, but evenings in his chambers grow lonely. He misses Agnes. After several days, Steerforth visits, and David impulsively invites him and two Oxford friends to dinner. The meal, prepared by the incompetent Mrs. Crupp through a pastrycook, includes various disasters, but David is determined to impress. His guests—Grainger and Markham—arrive, and David, nervous about appearing young, drinks too much wine. The evening becomes increasingly chaotic. David proposes extravagant toasts to Steerforth, becomes maudlin, drinks more, and eventually loses coherent control. Someone suggests the theatre. In David's drunken haze, they attend a performance where, to his horror, he encounters Agnes in a box with her friends. She's distressed by his condition and quietly begs him to leave for his sake. The next morning brings crushing shame. David suffers terrible physical and moral anguish, remembering "that indelible look which Agnes had given me." He can't contact her, doesn't know where she's staying, and feels he's betrayed everything good in their relationship. The following evening, Steerforth appears, and David confesses his wretchedness. Steerforth takes it lightly, but the incident has deepened David's sense of Agnes as his "good Angel" and moral compass. The chapter brilliantly captures the misery of youthful excess and the particular shame of disappointing someone whose good opinion matters most.

CHAPTER 25: XXV: Good and Bad Angels
Still suffering from shame, David receives a kind note from Agnes asking him to visit. At Mr. Waterbrook's house, David tearfully apologizes, and Agnes gently forgives him. She then warns him against Steerforth—his "bad Angel"—basing her judgment on Steerforth's character and influence over David. Though David defends Steerforth passionately, Agnes's words create a shadow over his feelings. Agnes reveals that Uriah Heep is entering partnership with her father, exploiting Mr. Wickfield's weaknesses until "papa is afraid of him." She accepted this sacrifice to relieve her father's distress. That evening, David attends a stiff dinner party at the Waterbrooks' where guests obsess over aristocratic "Blood." David happily reunites with Traddles, now reading for the bar. Afterward, David reluctantly invites Uriah home for coffee. At David's chambers, Uriah reveals he's in love with Agnes and intends to marry her, having wormed his way into the family's dependence. He asks David to keep his secret and not oppose him. David is horrified but powerless. Uriah, unable to return to his boarding house, sleeps before David's fire. David spends a nightmarish, sleepless night haunted by the image of this "red-headed animal" aspiring to Agnes, even checking on the sleeping Uriah multiple times in revulsion. The chapter powerfully contrasts Agnes's pure goodness with Uriah's repulsive manipulation, deepening the threat to the Wickfield household.

CHAPTER 26: XXVI: I Fall Into Captivity
Mr. Spenlow invites David to his home in Norwood. David arrives to meet Spenlow's daughter Dora—and instantly falls desperately in love. Dora is pretty, childlike, and accompanied by her little dog Jip. To David's shock, Dora's companion is Miss Murdstone, his childhood tormentor. Miss Murdstone coldly proposes they maintain distant civility given their history, and David agrees. But David barely notices, consumed by infatuation with Dora. That night he can only think of her "girlish, bright-eyed lovely" perfection. Next morning, David encounters Dora in the garden. She complains that Miss Murdstone is "so absurd" and talks only to her dog Jip, who jealously barks at David. At church, David hears nothing but Dora. Returning to London, David becomes obsessed. He buys expensive waistcoats, wears tight boots, and walks miles hoping to glimpse Dora. He haunts fashionable streets and shops. His work at Doctors' Commons becomes a daydream about Dora—he sees her name engraved on the silver oar of the Admiralty Court. Mrs. Crupp, his landlady, penetrates his secret, noting his changed eating and grooming habits as symptoms of love. She offers motherly advice to "cheer up" and suggests distracting himself with skittles. The chapter brilliantly captures the all-consuming, irrational nature of first love, with David completely transformed into a lovesick young man, his practical judgment entirely overthrown by passion for the enchanting but shallow Dora.

CHAPTER 27: XXVII: Tommy Traddles
David visits his old schoolfellow Traddles in Camden Town, finding him in modest lodgings above dubious neighbors. Traddles, cheerful despite poverty, is reading for the bar while doing legal copying and encyclopedia work to survive. He shares chambers with three others and employs a quarter-share of a clerk. Traddles reveals he's engaged to "the dearest girl"—a curate's daughter in Devonshire, one of ten children. They've begun accumulating furniture: a flowerpot and a small marble-topped table. Traddles explains they operate on the motto "Wait and hope," prepared for a long engagement. The landlords below are revealed to be the Micawbers. Mr. Micawber appears, unchanged in his theatrical eloquence and financial distress. Mrs. Micawber, "a little more slatternly," nearly faints with emotion at seeing David. The Micawbers are as impecunious as ever, with Mr. Micawber working on commission selling corn (unsuccessfully) while waiting for "something to turn up." They invite David to return for dinner. Later, Mr. Micawber confides he's engaged in corn sales and expecting a new addition to the family, while his wife's relations express "dissatisfaction," which Micawber repels "with scorn, and with defiance!" The chapter reunites David with two contrasting figures from his past: the steadfast, patient Traddles, whose good nature and optimism persist despite hardship, and the ever-hopeful, ever-impoverished Micawbers, whose cheerful resilience masks perpetual crisis. Both represent forms of honorable struggle against adversity.

CHAPTER 28: XXVIII: Mr. Micawber’s Gauntlet
David hosts dinner for the Micawbers and Traddles. Despite Mrs. Crupp's incompetence ruining the food, Mr. Micawber saves the meal by improvising "deviled" mutton on the gridiron, and the party becomes merry. Mrs. Micawber delivers a speech about her husband's prospects, dismissing corn and coals as "not remunerative." She proposes he "throw down the gauntlet to society" through advertisements, financed by raising money on a bill. After toasting "D." (Dora), Micawber slips David a desperate letter: he's "Crushed," a broker's man possesses his lodgings, and Traddles's £23 acceptance is unpaid. David warns Traddles about lending his name, but Traddles has already done so. Later, Steerforth arrives from Yarmouth with news: Mr. Barkis is dying. Steerforth has been secretly visiting Yarmouth and speaks of the Peggottys dismissively. He urges David to "Ride on over all obstacles" in life. David decides to visit dying Barkis. Steerforth invites him to Highgate first, enigmatically asking David to "stand between Rosa Dartle and me." They part warmly, Steerforth asking David to "think of me at my best" if they're ever separated. David nearly confesses his doubts but holds back out of loyalty to Agnes's confidence. Watching Steerforth walk home, David wishes "for the first time, that he had some worthy race to run," sensing something amiss beneath his friend's brilliance and restless energy.

CHAPTER 29: XXIX: I Visit Steerforth at His Home, Again
David visits Highgate before traveling to Yarmouth. Rosa Dartle watches him intensely, comparing his face with Steerforth's, searching for something. She confronts David privately, desperately asking what is leading Steerforth—"anger, hatred, pride, restlessness, wild fancy, love?" David honestly knows nothing. Rosa's scar throbs as she makes him swear secrecy. At dinner, Rosa speculates whether people of similar temperament would divide more deeply if they quarreled. Mrs. Steerforth dismisses any possibility of quarreling with her son. Throughout the day, Steerforth charms Rosa from sharpness into pleased companionship. That evening, Rosa plays harp and sings an unearthly Irish song with fearful passion. Steerforth puts his arm around her affectionately; Rosa strikes him "with the fury of a wild cat" and storms out. Mrs. Steerforth attributes this to Rosa's "soured temper." That night, Steerforth asks David to promise: "If anything should ever separate us, you must think of me at my best." David assures him of unchanging love. Next morning, David finds Steerforth sleeping peacefully and leaves. The chapter ends with David's ominous narration from the future: "Never more, oh God forgive you, Steerforth! to touch that passive hand in love and friendship. Never, never more!" The foreshadowing is unmistakable—something terrible is imminent, and this is David's last sight of his beloved friend in innocence and peace.

CHAPTER 30: XXX: A Loss
David arrives in Yarmouth and visits Mr. Omer, who reports Mr. Barkis is dying. Omer notes that Em'ly seems "unsettled"—still pretty and hardworking but lacking heart, clinging ever tighter to her uncle. David goes to the Peggotty household, where Em'ly sits trembling by the fire. When her uncle suggests she go home with Ham, she begs to stay, revealing deep distress despite Ham's gentle presence. Upstairs, Mr. Barkis lies dying, clutching his treasure box, muttering "Old clothes!" Mr. Peggotty explains the local belief: "People can't die, along the coast, except when the tide's pretty nigh out." As the tide ebbs, Barkis briefly revives, recognizes David, and speaks his final words: "Barkis is willin'!" With the outgoing tide, he dies. This chapter, though titled "A Loss," centers on Barkis's death, but the real loss is foreshadowed through Em'ly's strange behavior. Her trembling, her desperate clinging to her uncle, her tearful self-reproach about not being good enough—all signal her inner turmoil. The chapter's famous line "Barkis is willin'" echoes his marriage proposal to Peggotty, creating a poignant full-circle moment. The symbolism of dying with the tide reflects the natural rhythms of the coastal community. Em'ly's extreme distress, though attributed to death's presence, suggests a deeper crisis approaching. The chapter balances tenderness (Barkis's gentle death) with foreboding (Em'ly's inexplicable anguish).

CHAPTER 31: XXXI: A Greater Loss
David helps Peggotty with Barkis's affairs, discovering nearly £3,000 in his treasure box. Barkis left income to Mr. Peggotty for life, remainder to Peggotty, Em'ly, and David; the rest to Peggotty. After the quiet Blunderstone funeral, David joins the family at the boathouse. Mr. Peggotty cheerfully lights Em'ly's candle in the window. Ham arrives alone, deadly pale, with devastating news: Em'ly has run away. Her letter reveals she's gone to become "a lady," begging forgiveness and telling them to think of her as dead. Mr. Peggotty demands to know who took her. Ham reveals servants and a gentleman were seen with Em'ly at dawn on the Norwich road—the man is Steerforth. David is paralyzed by horror at his friend's betrayal. Mr. Peggotty announces he'll "seek my niece" across the world. Mrs. Gummidge, transformed by crisis, becomes strong and comforting rather than complaining. David's guilt overwhelms him—he introduced Steerforth to this innocent family, enabling this destruction. The "greater loss" encompasses Em'ly's ruin, Mr. Peggotty's shattered happiness, Ham's destroyed future, the family's honor, and David's friendship with Steerforth. The chapter ends with collective weeping as the magnitude of the catastrophe settles over them. David's trust and love have been weaponized against those he cares for, creating a devastating betrayal on multiple levels that will reverberate throughout the remainder of the novel.

CHAPTER 32: XXXII: The Beginning of a Long Journey
Despite Steerforth's betrayal, David finds he "never had loved Steerforth better than when the ties that bound me to him were broken," grieving for what his friend might have been. The town sympathizes with Mr. Peggotty and Ham. On the beach, Mr. Peggotty announces he'll seek Em'ly worldwide. He wants the old boat kept unchanged, candle lit nightly, so if she returns penitent, she'll find welcome. Ham will work and support the search with his savings. Mrs. Gummidge transforms into a capable, devoted caretaker. In London, David and Mr. Peggotty confront Mrs. Steerforth, who coldly refuses to allow Steerforth to marry Em'ly—such a match would disgrace him. Mr. Peggotty eloquently pleads, but she's adamant: Steerforth will receive no forgiveness unless he abandons Em'ly. Rosa Dartle viciously attacks them, wishing to see Em'ly "whipped," "branded," hunted "to her grave." Miss Mowcher appears at David's chambers, revealing she unwittingly helped Steerforth by delivering a letter, having been deceived. She vows to help find Em'ly. Mr. Peggotty departs alone on his quest with only a small sum and his stick, walking into the sunset with the words: "I'm a-going to seek her, fur and wide. If any hurt should come to me, remember that the last words I left for her was, 'My unchanged love is with my darling child, and I forgive her!'" His solitary figure becomes an enduring image of faithful, forgiving love.
